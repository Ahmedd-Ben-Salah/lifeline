Lifeline

The supply chain between worlds. AI agents forecast danger on Mars and move air, water and food between colonies — and from Earth — before anyone runs out.

This document explains, in simple words, what we built, how every number is computed, how the AI agents work, and how to answer the jury's questions. Everything here matches the code in the repository.

1. The pitch in one minute

Hook. In June 2018 a global dust storm cut the Opportunity rover's daily energy from about 645 Wh to 22 Wh in a week. It never woke up. Now imagine four cities on Mars.

Problem. Rockets can only leave Earth for Mars about every 26 months (the synodic period, 780 days). The trip takes 6–9 months. Messages take 3 to 22 minutes one way. A colony must order what it needs up to two years before it needs it — the longest supply chain humanity will ever run. Today nothing manages it: each colony watches itself, Earth reacts too late, and when a rover breaks nothing replans delivery.

Solution — three jobs. (1) Forecast storms at three horizons, like a weather service (Outlook ~18 months, Watch weeks, Track days with a hurricane-style cone). (2) Coordinate: one AI agent per colony plus weather, logistics and Earth-supply agents talk with a signed protocol (WARN → NEED → OFFER → ACCEPT, TIMEOUT → act alone, ORDER). (3) Deliver: rover convoys on real Mars terrain, rerouted on failure; Earth resupply timed to the launch window, packed and landed.

Golden rule. The math decides, the AI explains. Every number on screen comes from a deterministic engine. Agents only call tools and quote their results. Big actions need a human “Go”.

Close. “On Earth, a late delivery is annoying. On Mars, it is fatal. Lifeline makes sure help is already on its way.”

The story the agents play

  1. WARN weather-agent: S-17 formed over Hellas; 233/300 runs reach Arcadia between sol +8 and +14; solar loss up to ~75% for ~41 sols.
  2. NEED jezero-agent → arcadia-agent: to keep 30 days of reserve Arcadia needs ~4,240 kg water and ~1,370 kg O₂ (resource forecast, 300 runs).
  3. TIMEOUT Arcadia's antenna is down. No reply in 20 minutes (policy) → Jezero may act alone for life-critical actions.
  4. OFFER Utopia offers 1,550 kg water + 540 kg O₂ — only what keeps its own 90-day reserve — on rover r-7.
  5. ACCEPT Jezero accepts (policy: auto + notify humans, up to 2 t). Transfer TR-0921 is created.
  6. FAULT Rover r-3 (carrying 900 kg water to Arcadia) breaks down on the direct route. The router sends r-7 via r-3 to recover its cargo: +0.3 sols, ETA 7.9 sols — just before the storm's earliest arrival (8.1 sols). r-7 now carries 2.99 t of its 3 t limit.
  7. ORDER earth-supply-agent drafts ORD-0312: 3 ships, 261 t, for the 2052 window, landing at arcadia-south (score 0.84). Needs two human approvals.
  8. Approvals travel to Earth with the real light delay (scaled for the stage). When two “Go”s have reached Earth, the order is SENT.

3. How the app is built

Browser (Next.js 16 pages, React 19, three.js) → polls /api/state every 1.5 s → when the world version changes, each page refetches its own payload (/api/overview, /api/forecasts, …).
API routes check the login cookie (signed JWT) and the role, then read or change the world: one JSON document saved in Redis (Upstash) on Vercel, or a local file in development.
Engine (lib/engine): pure TypeScript functions, deterministic, unit-tested. Every number on screen is recomputed from the world by the engine at request time.
Agents (lib/agents): Claude (Anthropic Messages API with tool use) writes the agent messages and answers questions, calling engine functions as tools. If Claude is off, slow, or quotes a number not in the tool results, a scripted template with the same numbers is used.

Why it works on serverless (no background worker)

Agent events (a timeout after 20 minutes, a message arriving after the light delay) are stored as a schedule in the world. On every read, tick(now) applies the events whose time has come. Light-delayed messages simply carry a deliverAt time: the Earth side doesn't see a Mars message until it “arrives”.

Security & roles

Passwords are bcrypt hashes; sessions are HS256-signed JWTs in httpOnly cookies; the route proxy redirects signed-out users to /login. Every mutating API checks the role on the server (a VIEWER approving gets HTTP 403). Every agent message carries an HMAC-SHA256 signature of its body and payload; every human or demo action is written to an audit log.

4. The engine — every number explained

4.1 Where the planets are — ephemeris.ts

We use NASA JPL's “Approximate Positions of the Planets” (Keplerian elements + rates, valid 1800–2050). For a date we compute the mean anomaly, solve Kepler's equation M = E − e·sin E with Newton's method, and rotate into the ecliptic frame. Accuracy: about one arc-minute — thousands of km, which is negligible for windows and delays.

  • Light delay = |Earth − Mars| ÷ 299,792.458 km/s. Test: 31 Jul 2018 close approach = 57.6 million km ✓.
  • Mars season Ls = Mars' heliocentric longitude − 85.06°. Test: Ls ≈ 0 on 26 Dec 2022 (MY 37 equinox) ✓.
  • Sols and local time: Mars Sol Date (Allison & McEwen) and local mean solar time = MTC + longitude/15. Message times in the app are LMST at Jezero.
  • Day/night on Mars: the sub-solar point latitude = asin(sin 25.19° · sin Ls), longitude where local time is noon. The 3D globes light Mars from that real direction.
  • Earth rotation: Greenwich sidereal time; Mars rotation: IAU prime meridian W = 176.630° + 350.89198226°/day.

4.2 Launch windows — lambert.ts, windows.ts

Lambert's problem: “which orbit takes me from Earth's position on the launch day to Mars' position on the arrival day in exactly that time?” We solve it with the universal-variable method (Curtis, Algorithm 5.2) using bisection (it never diverges). The departure speed relative to Earth gives C3 = v∞², the launch energy. The porkchop plot is a grid of 120 departure dates × 72 flight times (8,640 Lambert solutions, ~0.2 s). The window is the date with the lowest C3 found by scanning ~26 months. Test: the real 2026 window comes out 30 Oct 2026 with C3 9.1 km²/s² — the range mission designers publish ✓. The transfer path is drawn by integrating the orbit (Runge–Kutta) from the Lambert departure velocity.

4.3 The storm forecast — storm.ts

An ensemble, like hurricane forecasting: 300 copies of the storm, each with slightly different drift direction (±19°), speed (log-normal around 680 km/sol), lifetime (~40 sols) and growth. Each copy moves along a great circle in 6-hour steps; a colony is “reached” when it falls inside the dusty radius. Results: probability per colony (runs that reach it ÷ 300), arrival histogram, the cone (directions containing 67% and 90% of the runs), optical depth τ at the colony. All random numbers come from a seeded generator (mulberry32, seed 4412): same seed → identical forecast (tested).

Solar loss = 1 − e−0.313(τ−0.5), calibrated on Opportunity in June 2018 (τ ≈ 10.8 → 645 Wh to 22 Wh, −97%).

4.4 How long colonies last — resources.ts

Crew needs per person per sol from NASA's BVAD: O₂ 0.84 kg, water 6.5 kg (2.5 drink/food + 4.0 hygiene), food 1.8 kg, 98% water recovery. Reserve days = stock ÷ daily use. The 60-sol forecast integrates the stock day by day for each of the 300 storm runs: power = nuclear + solar × solar factor(τ); a power shortfall throttles production with the life-critical order (O₂ protected first, then water, then food lighting). Rover deliveries are added on their arrival sol. We show the median and the 10–90% band, plus a “no action” line.

4.5 Rover routes — router.ts

A* search on a 1°×1° grid of real Mars elevation (MOLA). Cost = distance × (1 + slope penalty) + storm-cone penalty (cells inside the forecast cone at the time the rover would be there) + blocked zones. Speed 20 km/h, 18 h of driving per sol = 360 km/sol (autonomous nuclear rovers). The path is smoothed and its elevation profile is shown on the Logistics page. Test: Olympus Mons > 12 km, Hellas < −5 km ✓.

4.6 Earth order — packer.ts, landing.ts

Demand = each colony's structural deficit (use − production) × 780 days (until the following window) × 1.15 dust-season margin + spares + 2 replacement rovers. The packer splits it into tanks/pallets and fills 3 ships of 100 t (Starship-class assumption), life-critical cargo first and spread across ships (losing one ship never loses all the oxygen). Landing score = 0.35·(1 − slope/10°) + 0.25·(1 − quake risk) + 0.25·(1 − distance/2500 km) + 0.15·(1 − dust τ/5).

4.7 Outlook and Watch — season.ts

Outlook uses climatology: regional storms peak in three seasons each Mars year (A ~Ls 225°, B ~Ls 275°, C ~Ls 325°; Kass et al. 2016) and planet-encircling storms happened in 3 of 14 recorded Mars years (2001, 2007, 2018). Watch is a transparent heuristic (logistic on dust trend, area and season). We say clearly in the Q&A that these two are heuristics; the Track ensemble is the simulation.

5. The AI agents

Agents reason and talk; tools calculate. Claude never invents a number: it calls tools (engine functions) and may only quote their results. Code enforces it.
GuardrailHow it works in the code
Number groundingEvery number in Claude's reply must appear in the tool results of that conversation (numbersGrounded). If not → the reply is replaced by the scripted wording of the same facts.
Timeouts9 s per protocol message, 28 s per question → scripted fallback. The demo never waits on the network.
Human GoEarth orders need 2 approvals from 2 different people; approvals from Mars must physically reach Earth (light delay) before the order is SENT.
Policy autonomyEach action (warn, accept offer, dispatch rover, change rations, Earth order) is AUTO / NOTIFY / GO per comms state (normal, delayed, blackout). Editable on the Policies page.
Signatures & auditHMAC-SHA256 on every message; audit log for every human/demo action.
Refusal fallbackServer-side fallback model enabled on the Claude API call; on any error the scripted agent answers.

Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (claude-opus-5-5) at low effort for short protocol messages and questions; switchable to Sonnet 5.5 with one environment variable. Prompts, tools and roles are listed in docs/AGENT_PROMPTS.md.

Live replay (“Inject storm”): the engine computes the facts of all eight steps first; Claude writes all eight messages in parallel (≈ 3–6 s); they are then released one by one in real time (reply timeout scaled to ~24 s, Earth delay scaled to ~39 s). Each message shows whether Claude or the template wrote it.

Ask the agents: a real tool-use loop — e.g. “What if the storm lasts 60 sols?” makes the agent call resource_forecast(colony: arcadia, resource: O2, storm_duration_sols: 60) and answer with the run-out sol; tool chips under the answer show exactly which tools ran.

  • Real textures: Mars colour from the Viking mosaic; relief from MOLA elevation turned into a normal map and a shaded-relief map (our script scripts/build_textures.py); Earth from NASA Blue Marble (day) and Black Marble (night lights), ocean mask for sun glint, clouds; star background from the NASA Tycho star map; Phobos, Deimos from NASA 3D Resources.

7. What is real, what is simulated

Real
Planet positions (JPL), launch windows and C3 (Lambert), trajectories, light delay, Mars seasons and local time, true day/night, Earth and Mars rotation, Mars elevation (MOLA) for routing, colony coordinates, crew needs (BVAD), solar loss calibrated on Opportunity 2018, Phobos/Deimos periods, dust-season climatology.
Simulated
The 2050 colonies' stocks and sensors, the rover fleet and its speed, cargo ship size (100 t), storm S-17 and its ensemble parameters, the Watch heuristic. Planets in 3D are enlarged so you can see them; positions are true.

9. Q&A cheat sheet

Can you really predict a storm 18 months ahead?
No one can predict a specific storm that far. We forecast the season's risk 18 months ahead (that's the lead time Earth needs for an order), detect forming storms weeks ahead (Watch), and track them days ahead with probabilities (Track).
What if the AI hallucinates?
It can't change a number: the engine computes all of them. The code rejects any Claude reply containing a number that isn't in the tool results and uses a template instead. Big actions need two humans. Every message is signed and logged.
Why not let the AI decide everything?
Life-critical decisions need accountability. Our policy page sets, per action and per communication state, whether agents act automatically, notify humans or need a Go. During a blackout, more is automatic — because waiting 40 minutes for Earth can kill.
How do you know the launch window is right?
We solve Lambert's problem for every departure/arrival pair and pick the minimum launch energy. For 2026 we get 30 October with C3 ≈ 9 km²/s², matching published mission-design values. 21 unit tests check the engine against real references.
How accurate are the planet positions?
JPL's approximate elements: about one arc-minute for Earth and Mars in 1800–2050 — a few thousand km, irrelevant for windows and delays. Our scenario date (2050) is at the edge of the validity range; for later dates we would switch to JPL DE ephemerides.
Why 0.78?
233 of 300 ensemble runs bring the storm over Arcadia. Change the seed and you get a slightly different number; same seed always gives the same result so the demo is reproducible.
Where does “19 days” come from?
670 kg of O₂ ÷ 35.3 kg per sol (42 people × 0.84 kg/person/sol from NASA BVAD).
Why does the rover take 7.9 sols?
2,722 km on a route computed by A* over real Mars elevation, at 360 km per sol (20 km/h, 18 h/sol), plus 0.3 sols to load r-3's cargo.
What happens during solar conjunction (Earth silent ~2 weeks)?
The light-delay model and the autonomy matrix handle it: messages to Earth queue, and the “blackout” column of the policy decides what agents may do alone.
What's the business model?
SaaS per colony / per mission operator: forecasting + coordination + resupply planning seats for colony ops, mission control and suppliers (roles already in the product). Today's analogues: Antarctic stations, offshore platforms, remote mines and disaster logistics on Earth.
What would you build next?
Real wind fields from Mars climate models in the ensemble, JPL DE ephemerides, multi-revolution Lambert, battery models for rovers, and integrations with real telemetry.
Is the data stored securely?
Server-only API key, signed sessions, role checks on every API, audit log, signed messages. The world state lives in Redis (Upstash) in production.
What did you build vs borrow?
All app code, the engine, agents, UI and 3D scenes are ours. Borrowed: open-source libraries (Next.js, React, three.js, Anthropic SDK) and public NASA/USGS imagery, credited in the README.
